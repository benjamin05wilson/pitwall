## STATISTICS & CALIBRATION


**Q (warm-up). Walk me through why you fit per-driver fixed effects for tyre degradation instead of a naive pooled model. What exactly is the confound, and where does the FIA rulebook make it bite?**

A naive pool that ignores who ran what would confound the degradation slope k1 with car and driver pace. Concretely: if a quick car mostly ran the hard and a slow car mostly ran the soft, a pooled fit would read the cars' pace gap as a compound effect and corrupt both the k0 offset and the k1 slope. I give every driver their own intercept - in the pooled fit it's a per-(race,driver) intercept (the 'group' dummy, calibrate.py line 253) - so base pace is absorbed and k0/k1 are identified purely from within-driver variation as one driver ages one tyre across a stint. I also carry a per-race linear track-evolution term so rubbering-in isn't mistaken for negative degradation. The FIA two-compound rule is exactly what makes this bite: it forces different drivers onto different compound sequences, so the compound assignment is correlated with the field, and pooling without fixed effects is biased by construction. The honest caveat is that fixed effects only remove *additive* per-driver pace - if a driver is differentially harder on softs than hards, that interaction lands in the slope. I don't model driver-by-compound interactions, and with this data I couldn't identify them.


> *Likely follow-up:* If fixed effects absorb additive driver pace, how do you know your k1 isn't soaking up a driver-by-compound interaction (a tyre-aggressive driver who only ran softs)? Steer: concede it's a genuine identification limit, note the per-(race,driver) intercept plus pooling across races dilutes any single driver's pull, and that the RMSE ~0.5 s and recovered pecking order bound how large the leakage can be.


**Q (warm-up). You run a 1.07x per-group median pre-filter AND IRLS-Huber. That looks redundant. Defend keeping both, and tell me which one is doing the real work.**

They're not redundant because they're not symmetric. The 1.07x filter (calibrate.py line 141) is one-sided: it drops any lap slower than 1.07x that driver-compound's median, killing the SC/traffic/lift-and-coast slow laps that survived the green-flag TrackStatus filter. It does nothing to fast laps. IRLS-Huber (lines 207-213) is two-sided on residuals after the model is fit, with k=1.345 on a MAD scale, so it down-weights moderate outliers in either direction - including a suspiciously fast lap the median filter let through. Which does the real work? The pre-filter removes the gross contamination that would otherwise dominate a least-squares fit and even bias the MAD scale Huber relies on; Huber then cleans up the residual moderate stuff. If I had to drop one I'd keep the pre-filter, because an un-pre-filtered fit lets a cluster of SC laps shift the whole slope before Huber gets a chance. The honest weakness: a hard 1.07x threshold is a chosen constant, not learned, and on a circuit with genuinely high lap-to-lap spread it could clip real slow-tyre laps and flatten k1 slightly.


> *Likely follow-up:* Your 1.07x threshold is one-sided and fixed. On a high-deg circuit where late-stint laps are legitimately 8%+ off the stint median, doesn't it amputate exactly the degradation signal you're trying to measure? Steer: agree it's a risk, note the median is per-(driver,compound) not per-stint so it's anchored near mid-stint not fresh pace, and that you'd want to make the threshold compound-aware or switch to a purely Huber pass on high-spread circuits.


**Q (core). You shrink empirical SC probability toward a prior with K=4. Show me the actual numbers on a real circuit, and justify K=4 rather than just reporting the raw rate or trusting the prior.**

Take Monza: 0 of 3 recent races had a full SC, so raw is 0.0. Trusting that gives you a model that thinks Monza can never throw a safety car, which is absurd - it just hasn't in a 3-race window. The circuit prior is 0.27, and the conjugate update p=(p_raw*n + p_prior*K)/(n+K) gives (0*3 + 0.27*4)/7 = 0.154. Bahrain goes the other way: raw 0.333, prior 0.45, shrunk to 0.40. Jeddah raw 1.0 (all three had SC) shrinks to 0.829 - I don't let three races convince me a circuit is a certainty. K=4 means I weight the structural prior as four pseudo-races against my three real ones, so the prior modestly dominates at this sample size and the data takes over as races accumulate. I ship both p_sc_raw and p_sc in the JSON so a strategist sees exactly how much is data versus prior. The honest part: K=4 isn't tuned against any held-out metric - it's a regularisation choice, and I can defend the *order of magnitude* (not 1, not 20) but not 4.0 versus 5.0. The prior itself is the Heilmeier-era circuit profile, which is its own modelling assumption, so the floor on Monza is only as good as that 0.27.


> *Likely follow-up:* K=4 with a prior you can't independently validate just launders one assumption into another. How would you defend the Monza 0.154 to a strategist who's about to leave a stop in hand for a safety car that your model says is twice as likely as the data shows? Steer: be honest that you'd quote the raw 0.0 alongside, frame 0.154 as a deliberate floor against a 3-race blind spot, and note the decision is driven by the full SC-count/timing distribution and CVaR, not this single scalar - so a strategist sees the spread, not a point estimate.


**Q (core). Your reliability prior splits mechanical DNF from incidents. Quote me your actual numbers - including for Williams - and tell me why the split matters for a strategy call.**

I'll quote the real output, and I'm not going to flatter anyone: across 2023-25 Jolpica results the field overall mechanical p_dnf is 0.11, and Williams is the *worst* in the set at 0.209 mechanical DNF over 139 starts - higher than Alpine 0.136, well above Ferrari 0.093 and Haas 0.10. That's the calibration speaking, not a guess, and if I'm interviewing for Williams I'd rather show I read my own output than recite a flattering made-up number. The split (calibrate.py 457-466): _is_finish accepts Finished, Lapped, and '+N Lap'; of the retirements, _INCIDENT_STATUSES pulls out Accident, Collision, Spun off, DSQ, DNS and so on, and everything else - power unit, gearbox, hydraulics - is mechanical. It matters because mechanical failure is a property of the car and should price into a strategy's risk: a 0.21 mechanical rate means an aggressive 3-stop that maximises track position but adds pit-lane exposure and a hotter-run engine is a worse bet for this car than for Ferrari. Crashes are driver-and-circumstance noise; folding them into 'reliability' would punish a team for a first-lap tangle and corrupt the number I actually use in the Monte-Carlo DNF sampling. The honest limits: status strings are coarse - a hydraulic leak triggered by kerb damage from contact gets mislabelled mechanical, and a retirement for an ambiguous 'Power unit' could be a precaution not a failure; and 139 starts still leaves a wide interval on a 0.21 rate, with no formal CI shipped.


> *Likely follow-up:* 0.21 over 139 starts - what's the confidence interval, and is the Williams number even stationary given a 2025 reliability push would make 2023 data actively misleading? Steer: give the rough Wald/Wilson interval (~0.21 +/- 0.07), concede non-stationarity is the bigger problem, and say the right fix is recency-weighting or per-season fits so an in-season reliability improvement isn't masked by older failures.


**Q (core). In dirty-air you only use 'held-station' laps - gap under 3 s and not closing faster than 0.4 s/lap. Walk me through the bias if you dropped that filter, and then defend an r of 0.13-0.29 as worth shipping at all.**

Drop the filter and you poison the sign. A fast Ferrari catching a slow car logs laps *faster* than its own baseline while its gap-ahead is small - not because the wake helped, but because it's a quick car closing. The regression would read 'small gap correlates with fast lap' and hand you a negative wake coefficient: cars go faster in traffic. Nonsense. The held-station condition (fastf1_loader line 193: gap_ahead < 3.0 AND the gap not shrinking faster than 0.4 s/lap) keeps only laps where the car is genuinely stuck at roughly constant separation, so the proximity term max(0, gap0 - gap_ahead) measures pure pace lost to the wake. On the r: I ship it precisely because I'm not asking the linear term to explain the lap, only to estimate one coefficient. r of 0.13-0.29 means the wake explains 10-20% of held-lap variance - the rest is driver inputs, DRS, lift-and-coast, none of which I observe, so low r is expected. What earns the ship is sign, magnitude and ordering on large samples: Monaco 0.83 s/lap versus Monza 0.13 s/lap on 1516 and 1122 held laps respectively, which is exactly the high-downforce-versus-low-downforce wake story. I gate on theta>0 and n_held>=200 so a noisy or wrong-signed circuit fit never ships. The honest line: this is a credible *point estimate of a structural effect*, not a precise per-lap predictor, and it only moves under/overcut economics - I wouldn't bet a binary call on the third significant figure.


> *Likely follow-up:* Your 0.4 s/lap closing threshold is itself a free parameter - tighten it and you keep only dead-stuck laps (low n, less bias), loosen it and you readmit closers (more n, more bias). How did you pick 0.4, and does the Monaco>>Monza ordering survive moving it? Steer: admit 0.4 is a judgement call not a swept optimum, argue it's chosen to admit normal lap-to-lap gap jitter while excluding genuine catching, and commit that the right validation is a robustness sweep over the threshold showing the ordering is stable - which you'd run before trusting magnitudes.


**Q (KILLER). Your temperature coupling is fully built but shipped at 0.0. Convince me that's science and not just hiding a result you didn't like. What did the fit actually say, and what would make you ship it?**

The fit said r=-0.553 over 45 (compound,race) points - that's in priors.json, not hidden. The two-stage estimator (calibrate.py 651-697) fits each race's per-compound k1 independently in stage one, so circuit asphalt and evolution are absorbed, then regresses those slopes on race-mean track temp with compound fixed effects in stage two, so the coefficient is the within-compound thermal sensitivity. The physical prior is unambiguous: hotter rubber degrades faster, so the slope must be positive. I got a moderately strong *negative* slope. There is no honest world in which I ship a coefficient that says cold tracks wear tyres faster - it would actively make every hot-race strategy worse. So build_temp_coupling (line 114) holds any non-positive estimate at 0.0 and logs it. Why is the fit wrong rather than the physics? Three confounds I can name and can't remove at this resolution: Pirelli re-specs compounds year-to-year so the 'Soft' label isn't constant across my pooled years; hot races skew toward particular circuit/setup regimes; and aggregating S/M/H smears three different thermal responses into one slope. I'd ship it the moment I can fit within a single construction year per compound, or when I have tyre-surface temps instead of track-air temps - the slope() path is already native-consistent and live, so it's a coefficient swap, not a rebuild. Shipping inert here is the disciplined call; the cost is the model is currently thermally flat, which I'd flag to any strategist using it across a big ambient swing.


> *Likely follow-up:* If your own estimator is this confounded that it flips the sign of a first-principles effect, why should I trust the *other* coefficients it produces - your k1 slopes come from the same machinery. Steer: distinguish sharply - k1 is identified from abundant within-stint within-driver variation (strong design), temp_coeff from 45 cross-race points across re-specs (weak design); the difference is data leverage, not estimator validity, and the RMSE ~0.5 s plus recovered pecking order is direct evidence k1 is sound.


**Q (KILLER). You ship wet floors of 9.23 s for inters and 18.54 s for full wets off two races. That's almost no data. Why isn't this just made-up, and where exactly is it most fragile?**

First, a correction I want on the record because it changes the risk picture: it's not 31 laps total. The intermediate floor (9.23 s) is built on 471 intermediate laps; only the full-wet floor (18.54 s) rests on the thin 31 full-wet laps, both across two races - Zandvoort 2023 and Monaco 2023. So the fragility is concentrated and I can name it: the full-wet number is the weak one, the intermediate number is reasonably sampled. The method (calibrate.py 512-558) loads every wet stint and uses *each race's own dry slick laps* as the baseline, so circuit and setup cancel and I'm measuring a within-race wet-minus-dry deficit, not comparing across circuits. I take the 10th-percentile fuel-corrected lap per compound as best-case pace, because wet running is chaotic and I want the representative quick laps, not the safety-car crawl. What keeps this honest rather than made-up: I surface n_inter, n_wet and n_races in the store so nobody mistakes 31 full-wet laps for a tight estimate, the bowl curvature is an explicit physical prior I never claimed to fit, and the model's *ordering* validates out of sample - the wet DP calls VER's real intermediate switch at Zandvoort L62 and Monaco 2023 L56 to the lap. That tells me the crossover - which is what a strategist actually acts on - is right even where the absolute full-wet magnitude has a wide interval. The failure mode I'd warn a strategist about: don't trust the 18.54 s full-wet floor in a steady-state monsoon I haven't seen; both my races were transitional. The mitigation is structural - the live layer re-fits on every wet race, so the posterior tightens through a season.


> *Likely follow-up:* If the full-wet floor is essentially unidentified, why ship a number at all rather than fall back to a pure physical prior - and doesn't 'the crossover is right' just mean your *intermediate* floor and your prior bowl are doing all the work, with the full-wet number along for the ride? Steer: concede that's largely true and it's a feature - the inter floor (471 laps) plus the prior optima drive the actionable inter<->slick crossover, the full-wet floor mainly sets a rarely-hit inter<->wet boundary, and you'd happily replace it with the prior until more full-wet data lands.


**Q (KILLER). Your fuel correction bakes in k_fuel=0.030 s/kg before you ever fit. If the truth is 0.020 or 0.040, your k1 is wrong. Quantify the damage and tell me why you haven't checked.**

It's a real conditional dependency and I'll quantify it on Bahrain. Burn there derives to ~1.9 kg/lap over 57 laps, so a 20-lap stint sees a ~39 kg fuel swing. The correction I subtract scales linearly with k_fuel, so the *span* of correction across a 20-lap stint is 0.77 s at 0.020, 1.16 s at 0.030, and 1.54 s at 0.040. The thing that biases k1 is the difference: a 0.010 s/kg error mis-corrects the stint endpoints by about 0.39 s relative to each other, and since k1 is the slope over ~20 laps that's roughly a 0.02 s/lap bias on a slope that's order 0.05-0.06 s/lap - so yes, a 0.010 error is a meaningful fraction of the slope, and the sign is intuitive: over-correcting (k_fuel too high) makes the stint look like it degrades less, flattening k1. Why I'm relaxed but not complacent: 0.030 is the published consensus and sits mid-range of the cited 0.019-0.036 band, and crucially the *same* fuel model runs in calibration, simulation, ghost and backtest, so the engine is self-consistent and the bias largely cancels in strategy *deltas* - which is the quantity I optimise on, not absolute k1. Where I won't bluff: I have not run a k1-versus-fuel-model sensitivity sweep. It's cheap - re-fit at 0.020/0.030/0.040 and report the k1 band - and I should have it before quoting k1 to three figures. Until then the honest statement is that k1 is reported conditional on the fuel model and the RMSE folds in that model error.


> *Likely follow-up:* If the bias mostly cancels in deltas, give me a case where it does NOT cancel and actually flips a decision. Steer: the asymmetric case - comparing a long-stint one-stop against a short-stint two-stop, where the one-stop accrues far more fuel-correction error, so a mis-specified k_fuel biases the stop-count comparison, not just absolute pace; that's exactly the comparison the sensitivity sweep should stress.


**Q (KILLER). Across this whole calibration layer you ship no confidence intervals - point estimates for k1, SC probability, dirty air, wet floors, reliability. For a strategy tool where the value is in the risk tails, isn't that the real hole?**

Yes - that's the most honest single criticism of the stats layer and I won't wave it away. I report RMSE, sample sizes (n_held, n_inter, n_wet, n_races, starts) and the raw-versus-shrunk SC pair, but none of the shipped scalars carry a formal interval, so a downstream consumer can't see that Williams' 0.21 DNF is +/- ~0.07, or that the full-wet floor is far wider than the inter floor. The partial defence is that the *strategy* layer doesn't collapse to these point estimates: the safety car enters the optimiser as a full count/timing/duration distribution, DNF and pit variance are sampled in the Monte-Carlo, and selection is on a CVaR risk frontier under common random numbers - so the engine already reasons about tails, just not about *parameter* uncertainty on top of outcome uncertainty. That's the gap: I propagate outcome risk, not estimation risk. The fix is well-defined and I'd prioritise it - block-bootstrap the lap data for k1 intervals, Beta-Binomial posteriors on the SC and DNF rates (the K=4 shrinkage is already half a Bayesian step), and bootstrap the held-lap and wet samples - then push those as distributions into the same MC rather than fixed constants. The reason it isn't done is scope and runtime honesty, not because I think it's optional; I'd call it the top item on the calibration backlog.


> *Likely follow-up:* Concretely - would shipping parameter CIs actually change a single recommendation your optimiser makes today, or is it just hygiene? Steer: claim it would, and name where - thin-data circuits and wet calls, where wide parameter intervals would widen the CVaR tail enough to flip a marginal aggressive plan to a conservative one; commit to demonstrating it on one case rather than asserting it generally.

## CAUSAL IDENTIFICATION


**Q (core). Show me how the calibrated number is actually consumed in the simulator. Is the linear theta·(gap) law what the sim uses?**

No, and this is an important honesty point that an earlier draft glossed. The fit is linear in closeness — theta times max(0, gap0 - gap) — but the sim does NOT apply that linear law. In race.py:237-238 and 253-254 the sim adds a flat dirty_air_loss_s whenever the on-track gap is below dirty_air_gap = 1.0s (params.py:292), as a step function, not a ramp. The single number it adds is the loss the calibration evaluated at rep_gap = 0.7s (calibrate.py:642). So there are two deliberate simplifications: (1) I collapse the continuous penalty to a step, because the sim's track-position model already discretises following into 'in the window / not', and (2) the deployment trigger gap is 1.0s while the calibration window gap0 is 2.5s — they are not the same number. They are roughly reconciled because 0.7s sits inside the 1.0s trigger, so the magnitude I ship is the penalty at a gap the sim will actually see. But a strategist is right to flag that a car at 0.95s and a car at 0.1s get the identical tax in the sim, which the underlying fit says is wrong. Making the sim honor the linear ramp is a small change; I chose the step for speed and because the track-position clamp dominates the dynamics. There is also a hard clamp min(loss, 1.2) on the calibrated value (calibrate.py:647) so a pathological fit cannot inject an absurd penalty.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'A step penalty at the window boundary creates a discontinuity a car could game — does your optimiser exploit it?' Steer: in principle yes, a plan could sit a hair outside 1.0s to dodge the whole tax; in practice the optimiser places pit laps, not target gaps, so it cannot servo to the boundary, and the track-position clamp keeps gaps emergent not chosen. Concede the ramp would be cleaner and I would switch to it before trusting fine gap-level decisions.


**Q (core). How sensitive is theta to the held-station thresholds (gap < 3.0s, closing < 0.4 s/lap)? Have you swept them?**

I have not run a sensitivity sweep in code — there is no grid search over thresholds anywhere in calibrate.py, so that is an honest gap, not something I will dress up. What I can say from the structure: the proximity term itself uses gap0 = 2.5s (calibrate.py:561) as the window, while the held *flag* uses 3.0s (fastf1_loader.py:193), so a lap between 2.5 and 3.0s is flagged held but contributes prox=0 — it is effectively a clean-air control inside the held set, which makes the estimate fairly insensitive to the exact held cutoff between 2.5 and 3.0. The closing-rate clause at 0.4 s/lap is the consequential knob: tighten it and you keep only the most rigidly-stuck laps (less data, possibly larger theta), loosen it and you re-admit catching laps that bias theta down. I would expect the cross-circuit *ordering* to survive any reasonable setting because it is driven by aero regime, but I will not assert the *magnitudes* are stable to ±20% without running the sweep. The right deliverable is a small grid over (gap0, closing_rate) reporting theta and its bootstrap SE per circuit; I would prioritise that before quoting any single loss to a race engineer as decision-grade.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'Which threshold would you trust least and fix first?' Steer: the 0.4 s/lap closing rate — it is the one doing the causal work and it is the least empirically grounded (it is a heuristic regime boundary, not measured). I would replace the fixed cutoff with a per-circuit value derived from the distribution of lap-to-lap gap changes, or better, condition on actual closing speed from telemetry rather than lap-discretised gap deltas.


**Q (core). What endogeneity survives the held-station filter, and how big is it?**

Three residual concerns, in order of how much they worry me. (1) Tyre management in a train: a driver stuck behind someone may deliberately lift to save tyres rather than because of aero wake — the held filter cannot distinguish 'lost time to dirty air' from 'chose to back off', and both land in theta. This is the one I genuinely cannot rule out and it biases theta *up*. (2) Driver traffic skill: the per-(race,driver) dummy absorbs the *level* of a driver's pace but not his *slope* in traffic, so theta is a population-average wake penalty marginalised over racecraft — Verstappen and a rookie get the same number. For a strategy engine that wants a circuit-level penalty that is the right object, but I should call it an average, not a universal constant. (3) Gap measurement error from per-lap Position+Time snapshots plus the same-car-ahead identity bug I flagged — this is classic regressor noise and attenuates theta toward zero, partially offsetting (1). I have no formal interval, so my honest statement is that the Monaco 0.83s figure is a point estimate with a real but unquantified band, and the defensible claim is the *ordering* and the *order of magnitude*, not three significant figures.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'You said (1) biases up and (3) biases down — so the net sign is unknown. Why ship a point estimate at all?' Steer: because the strategy decision (is pitting for clear air worth a stop) is robust to that band — at Monaco even half of 0.83s flips the call, at Monza even double of 0.13s does not. I ship the point estimate but the *decision* only uses its coarse magnitude; I would not ship it for anything that needs the second decimal.


**Q (KILLER). Walk me through your dirty-air calibration. How do you know your theta coefficient measures the wake penalty and not just that slow cars sit in traffic?**

The identification rests on two things, and I want to be precise about both. First, per-(race,driver) fixed effects. In calibrate.py:619-634 every lap carries a dummy for its race-driver group, so a car's base pace is fully absorbed before theta sees anything — a slow car sitting in traffic contributes nothing to theta because its slowness is soaked into its own dummy. theta is identified purely from *within-driver* variation: the same driver, same compound, same race, comparing laps in clean air vs laps stuck behind someone. Second, the held-station filter (fastf1_loader.py:189-193): I keep a proximity lap only if gap < 3.0s AND the gap is not shrinking faster than 0.4 s/lap. That second clause is the one that matters — it drops laps where a faster car is reeling in a slower one, because on those laps the follower's own pace advantage would mask the wake tax. The proximity regressor itself is max(0, gap0 - gap_ahead) with gap0=2.5s (calibrate.py:595), so it's the seconds-of-closeness inside a 2.5s window, evaluated only on held laps. The fitted loss at a representative 0.7s following gap is 0.13s at Monza up to 0.83s at Monaco. I will not oversell the precision: the partial r of the proximity column is only 0.13-0.29, so theta is a real but noisily-estimated effect, and what gives me confidence it is signal not artefact is the cross-circuit ordering, which I will defend separately.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'Fixed effects absorb the level, but if the *fast* drivers spend more time in clean air and the *slow* ones in traffic, isn't theta still picking up a pace-traffic correlation within driver?' Steer: no — within a single driver-race dummy the level is gone; the residual correlation would need a driver to be systematically slower on his *own* close-following laps for a non-wake reason, which is exactly what the closing-rate clause removes. Concede it cannot remove a driver lifting to manage tyres in a train; that bias is bounded and I report it.


**Q (KILLER). You keep quoting r = 0.13-0.29 per circuit. Where is that stored, and what exactly is that r the correlation of?**

Honest answer first: it is NOT stored. priors.json persists only two dirty-air fields per circuit — dirty_air_loss_s and dirty_air_n (the held-lap count) — see priors.json and build_priors.py:86-87. The r and theta come back from calibrate_dirty_air (calibrate.py:647) but the build script deliberately does not write them, so I am quoting numbers I observed at fit time, not values you can read out of the shipped store. I would not pretend otherwise in the panel. What the r measures is also narrower than 'model fit': it is the simple sign-aware correlation between the proximity column and the raw fuel-corrected lap time across all laps (calibrate.py:644-646), NOT a partial correlation against the post-fixed-effects residual. So it is actually a conservative, slightly pessimistic diagnostic — most laps have prox=0 (clean air), which dilutes it toward zero. That is why I lean on it only as a sanity flag and gate on sign and sample size in the build, not on r. If a strategist wanted r reported per circuit in the store, that is a ten-line change and I would add it; its absence today is a real gap.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'If r isn't a partial correlation, you don't actually have a clean per-circuit fit statistic for theta at all — so how do you know theta is significant?' Steer: concede I have no per-circuit standard error in code. My evidence for significance is (a) the build gate requires theta>0 and n_held>=200, (b) the sign and cross-circuit ranking are stable across three independent seasons, and (c) the magnitudes track aero physics. That is informal, not a t-test; I would add a bootstrap over held-lap subsets to get a real SE.


**Q (KILLER). Your r is modest. Walk me through why the cross-circuit ordering should convince me theta is signal, not six independent noise draws that happened to rank plausibly.**

The ordering is the strongest evidence I have, and it is not a coincidence of six draws. Monaco 0.83, Saudi 0.43, Bahrain 0.35, Japan 0.26, Canada 0.25, Monza 0.13 (priors.json). That ranking lines up with aero regime: Monaco is maximum-downforce, low-speed, where a car lives in the leader's wake through a sequence of slow corners and loses front-end the whole lap; Monza is minimum-downforce with long straights and heavy braking zones where you can hang back and attack under braking, so the time-averaged wake tax is small. Each circuit is fit on a *separate* three-season pool of 880-1500 held laps, so these are six independent estimates, and the probability that six independent noise draws reproduce the textbook aero-sensitivity ordering is low. I want to correct one thing from an earlier draft: the circuits are NOT pooled together into one big regression — each is its own fit (build_priors.py iterates per circuit), so I cannot claim a single 4000-lap pooled estimate; I claim six independent ones that agree with physics. The honest limit: ordering being right does not prove each *magnitude* is right to within a tenth — only that the cross-sectional structure is real.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'p_sc at Saudi and Canada is high — could your dirty-air number there be contaminated by safety-car restarts bunching the field?' Steer: held laps are drawn only from green-flag, IsAccurate, TrackStatus=='1' laps (load_clean_laps, fastf1_loader.py:85-86), so SC laps are excluded before traffic annotation. Restart *out-laps* can still slip in as legitimately-close green laps, which would inflate the estimate slightly — concede that and note it as a refinement (drop N laps after every green flag).


**Q (KILLER). Your held-station filter compares this lap's gap to last lap's gap. Does it actually verify it's the *same car* ahead on both laps?**

No — and this is a genuine weakness I will not paper over. In load_laps_with_traffic (fastf1_loader.py:185-193) g_now is this car's gap to whoever is one position ahead this lap, and g_prev is its gap to whoever was ahead last lap. The delta = g_now - g_prev that defines 'held' assumes the car ahead is the same identity across the two laps, but the code never checks the identity. So if the car ahead pits, retires, or is overtaken, 'the car ahead' changes between laps and the delta is meaningless for that lap — it could falsely pass or falsely fail the held test. In practice this corrupts a minority of laps: pit cycles touch each car roughly once or twice a race, and a changed car-ahead usually produces a large gap jump that pushes the lap out of the <3.0s window anyway. But I cannot claim it is clean. The correct fix is to carry the car-ahead's driver ID per lap and require the same ID across the consecutive laps (and ideally >=3 consecutive held laps behind the same car). That is a known TODO, not something I have implemented, and with ~1000 held laps per circuit a few percent of misclassified laps is tolerable but not zero.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'If a few percent are misclassified, in which direction does it bias theta?' Steer: roughly toward zero / attenuation — misclassified laps inject gap noise uncorrelated with the true wake, classic measurement error in the regressor, which attenuates theta. So the bias if anything makes my reported losses conservative, not inflated; concede I have not proven the noise is uncorrelated with pace.

## SIMULATION & OPTIMISATION


**Q (warm-up). The robust optimiser exposes a Pareto frontier of expected position versus tail risk (CVaR). Walk me through exactly how the frontier is built, and where it is and is NOT used.**

CVaR at alpha=0.10 (EnsembleResult.cvar_position) is the mean finishing position over the worst 10% of scenarios -- a tail-risk number. The frontier (robust.py OptimizeResult.frontier) sorts candidates by expected position ascending, then walks them keeping a candidate only if its tail CVaR is strictly better than the best tail seen so far. That yields the Pareto set in (expected, tail) where you cannot improve the tail without conceding expected position. Concretely a 2-stop might sit at E[pos]=3.0 with CVaR 8.0 because a botched pit cycle occasionally buries it, while a 1-stop is E[pos]=3.5 with CVaR 5.0 because it has no second pit to go wrong; both make the frontier and the risk-appetite slider picks. The honest scoping point the first draft got wrong: this frontier is a Stage-2 / dashboard construct over the MC ensemble. The LIVE re-optimiser (live_optimizer.reoptimize) does NOT use the ensemble or CVaR at all -- it is a deterministic free-track remaining-time minimisation via prefix sums, and it ranks plans by expected remaining green-flag time with a cheap-pit window under SC/VSC. So live pit calls do not bake in SC/traffic tail exposure implicitly; they optimise expected time and react to the regime that has actually occurred. If a panellist asks 'does your live call account for the chance of a future SC', the honest answer is no -- it reacts to SCs, it does not price unrealised ones.


> *Likely follow-up:* So your live engine is risk-neutral while your offline one is risk-aware -- a strategist could get a 'BOX NOW' that is expected-optimal but tail-fragile. Isn't that a real inconsistency? Steer: own it. The deterministic live sweep trades risk-awareness for sub-millisecond latency and O(1)/plan; the safety net is that it reruns every lap off the live belief, so a bad tail tends to reveal itself and get corrected next lap rather than being committed once. A genuine fix is a CVaR-aware live objective, which costs an MC pass per lap I have not yet bought.


**Q (core). You call common random numbers (CRN) the single most important trick for turning a noisy simulator into a reliable optimiser. Explain precisely what is shared, what is not, and why that makes a comparison trustworthy.**

CRN works because the only thing I want to vary between two strategies is the strategy itself, not the luck of the draw. Concretely, a ScenarioSet (monte_carlo.py) is sampled once: each Scenario holds a full per-lap RaceControl timeline (green/SC/VSC/RED, baked in by SafetyCarSampler.sample) plus one integer seed. When I evaluate a strategy, the loop reseeds numpy's default_rng(scen.seed) per scenario and runs run_once, so the within-run stochastics -- per-lap pace noise and the stochastic mechanical retirements -- are redrawn identically for every candidate on that scenario. So both the SC timeline AND the noise stream are shared; what changes is only the pit laps and compounds. The delta in finishing position between strategy A and B on a given scenario is therefore attributable to strategy, and the noise is positively correlated across A and B so it cancels in the difference. The payoff is variance reduction on the comparison, not on the absolute: Var(A-B) = VarA + VarB - 2Cov(A,B), and CRN drives that covariance up. That is why 300-500 scenarios give a stable rank-ordering that would otherwise need thousands. One honest caveat: CRN reduces variance on the comparison but does nothing for bias -- if my SC hazard rate or pit-loss mean is off, every strategy inherits that error identically, so CRN makes me confident in the ranking, not in the absolute finishing positions.


> *Likely follow-up:* If a strategy pits one lap earlier and that desynchronises which RNG draws map to which lap, doesn't CRN partially break? Steer: concede it -- CRN is strongest when the random stream is indexed by scenario not by decision; a one-lap pit shift does reshuffle which noise sample lands on which lap, so the cancellation is imperfect. It still dominates independent sampling because the SC timeline (the dominant variance driver) is identical, but I would not claim perfect synchronisation.


**Q (core). Your deterministic DP for pit-lap placement is described as relying on convex tyre degradation. Prove it returns the true optimum, and tell me honestly what role convexity actually plays.**

I want to correct the framing first: it is not a greedy algorithm and it does not depend on convexity for correctness. _optimal_boundaries (deterministic.py) is an exact shortest-path DP. f[k][l] is the minimum green-flag time to cover laps 1..l with exactly k stints, and the recurrence min over every previous boundary lp of f[k-1][lp] + pit_loss + stint_cost[c][lp+1..l] enumerates ALL predecessors, not a locally greedy one. Optimality is the standard DP argument: an optimal k-stint plan ending at l must have an optimal (k-1)-stint prefix ending at its last pit, because stint costs are additive and the pit loss is a constant per stop -- so no exchange of an earlier boundary can improve a fixed suffix. That holds for ANY stint-cost table, convex or not. What convexity actually buys me is a different thing: it guarantees the optimum is well-behaved (interior, unique-ish) and explains the physics -- the cost table is built from green_lap = k0 + k1*age + k2*age^2, and the only reason a fresh tyre beats extending an old one is the positive slope plus curvature. I should be blunt that in the shipped pipeline the calibration is effectively LINEAR: fit_compounds_pooled sets k2=0, and fit_compounds only fits k2 when quadratic=True, which I do not use by default because I do not have enough laps to identify a cliff cleanly. So the k2 in CompoundParams is a small hand-set prior (~0.0003-0.0011 s/lap^2), not calibrated. The DP is exact regardless; the convexity story is mostly interpretive, and I would rather say that than oversell a fitted cliff I do not have.


> *Likely follow-up:* If the real tyre has a cliff your linear fit misses, your DP will run stints too long. How do you protect against that? Steer: the deterministic plan is only Stage 1 -- a shortlist generator; Stage 2 re-scores under MC with the prior k2 active in the lap-time model, and the live re-optimiser reprices the current stint off the Kalman degradation belief, so a real cliff shows up live as rising b and pulls the stop forward. I do not rely on the deterministic optimum surviving to the flag.


**Q (core). Your overtaking model still uses uncalibrated 2019 TUM constants (p_overtake=0.25, threshold_s=1.8, duel_penalty=0.3). Why, and what does that do to the strategies the engine proposes?**

I kept the TUM priors because p_overtake is weakly identified from the data I have. To fit it I would need, for every on-track duel, to know whether the trailing car actually had a pace advantage exceeding threshold on that lap and whether a pass was attempted versus a gap that just sat there -- which means lap-by-lap telemetry reconstruction plus filtering DRS, SC restarts and pit cycles. With weak signal I would overfit, so I left a credible prior from real 2018-19 racing. Mechanistically these live in _resolve_positions (race.py): a trailing car passes only if pace_adv >= threshold (lowered by DRS and a top-speed proxy) AND rng.random() < p_overtake; otherwise it is clamped to min_follow_gap and both cars eat a duel penalty, and the trailing car additionally pays the per-circuit dirty-air loss. The strategic consequence is that the engine's valuation of track position versus clear air is only as good as these constants: if real p_overtake is higher than 0.25, I over-value staying out in clean air and under-value an undercut; if lower, the reverse. The mitigation is empirical -- backtest Spearman ~0.72 on reconstructed races says the pecking order survives, so the miscalibration is second-order for ORDER even if it moves individual gap-management calls. I will not claim the risk profile around a clear-air pit is precisely calibrated; it is not.


> *Likely follow-up:* 2025 ground-effect cars follow much closer than 2019 -- your prior is from a different aero regime. Isn't 0.25 just wrong now? Steer: agree it is plausibly stale and in the wrong direction (closer following should raise both p_overtake and lower the effective threshold). I would frame the TUM value as a conservative anchor and flag that the right fix is a per-era refit from FIA position-delta + DRS logs; absent that I keep the strategy bias conservative rather than pretend the constant is current.


**Q (core). Your wet strategy is validated on 31 wet laps across 2 races. How credible is that calibration, and what does the model assume about observing track wetness?**

I will not dress this up: 31 wet laps from two 2023 races (Netherlands, Monaco) is sparse, and most seasons have zero fully wet races. What IS fitted honestly is the absolute pace deficit: calibrate_wet takes best-pace quantiles on inter/wet laps, fuel-corrected, against each race's own dry slick laps as the baseline, tyre-count weighted -- giving inter_floor 9.23s and wet_floor 18.54s. What is NOT fitted is the wetness-response curvature (inter_opt, inter_curv ~40 s/unit-w^2); that stays a physical convex prior because I have no track-wetness sensor to identify a curve from. The DP (optimal_wet_strategy) assumes a given wetness[lap] timeline, which I derive as a proxy from rainfall flags and the field's tyre mix -- a real limitation, because actual wetness is spatially non-uniform (one sector wet, another dry) and evolves with the drying line. The validation I lean on is behavioural, not statistical: the DP calls VER's actual inter switch at Zandvoort L62 and Monaco 2023 L56. That is two anecdotes, not a distribution, so I treat it as 'the proxy is good enough to find the crossover' rather than 'the wet model is calibrated'. I would not extrapolate it to mixed-condition strategy beyond the switch lap.


> *Likely follow-up:* Calling two real switch laps could be luck or could be the proxy leaking the answer -- the tyre-mix you build wetness from already encodes when the field switched. Isn't that circular? Steer: this is the sharpest hit and I concede partial circularity. The wetness timeline is partly derived from field tyre choices, so 'predicting' the crossover is not fully independent of it. The defensible claim is narrower: given a wetness curve, the DP picks a sensible switch; the curve itself is not independently observed. A clean test needs an exogenous moisture signal, which I do not have.


**Q (core). You report backtest Spearman ~0.72 versus real results. Tell me exactly what goes into that number, what it does and does not control for, and its failure modes.**

reconstruct_race (backtest.py) takes a real race, gives every driver their real grid, real stint sequence extracted from FastF1, and calibrated pace delta, runs 300 CRN scenarios, and ranks drivers by mean predicted position; Spearman 0.72 is the rank correlation of that order against the official classification. Two things I must be precise about. First, the sim does NOT force real retirements -- I want to correct any impression otherwise. DNFs only appear on the ACTUAL side: a real DNF gets position 99 when I sort the official result, but in the simulation the car runs its full strategy and only retires if the stochastic mechanical model happens to fire. So a driver who really blew an engine on lap 5 is, in most sim scenarios, classified as a finisher -- which actively HURTS the correlation and is a known confound, not something I am hiding. (Forcing real retirements with CRN is the ghost's job, not the backtest's.) Second, it is a single aggregate number over the races I have run; it does not break down by circuit, weather, or field spread, so I cannot yet say where the model is systematically wrong. The other failure modes are late red flags / SCs that the per-race timeline either had or did not, and rivals' strategies being their real ones rather than optimised. The metric I trust more for the product claim is podium accuracy, because mid-field rank churn -- where a tenth reshuffles three cars -- penalises Spearman without meaning the strategy logic is wrong.


> *Likely follow-up:* If real DNFs are scored 99 on the actual side but run to the flag in the sim, your 0.72 is contaminated by reliability noise that has nothing to do with strategy. Why quote it as strategy validation? Steer: agree it conflates strategy skill with reliability mismatch and therefore understates pure strategy fidelity. The clean version excludes or right-censors real DNFs (or forces them, as the ghost does) and reports Spearman on classified finishers only; I would present 0.72 as a conservative floor, not a headline.


**Q (KILLER). You claim the Rust accelerator achieves parity with Python within Monte-Carlo error, yet the RNG streams differ across languages. Be precise: what does parity mean, what tolerances, and how is it validated?**

Parity is explicitly NOT bit-for-bit. native.py says so in its docstring: the Rust loop reproduces RaceSimulator.run_once as a drop-in accelerator, but Python uses numpy PCG64 and Rust uses its own RNG, so per-lap noise and retirement timings differ run-for-run even on the same seed. What I validate is distributional agreement on the focal car. test_native.py runs a 1500-scenario ensemble on Bahrain with the focal 3rd on grid, 0.3s off pace, and asserts the Python and Rust mean finishing position agree within 0.25 positions and podium probability within 0.05. Those tolerances are deliberately loose because they have to clear the MC noise floor itself: at n=1500 the standard error on mean position is roughly sigma/sqrt(1500), and with a position spread of a few places that is on the order of 0.05-0.1 positions per estimate, so a 0.25 gate is a few sigma of headroom -- tight enough to catch a real logic divergence, loose enough not to flag RNG-stream differences. The honest limit: this is a single circuit and single grid/pace configuration; it confirms the two share the same MODEL (same degradation, fuel, pit, overtake equations -- and I pass the same effective thermal-aware k1 slope into Rust so they cannot silently diverge on tyre model), but it does not prove parity across all circuits or extreme configs. That is why the pure-Python sim is the declared source of truth for any policy decision, and Rust is only used where I want batch throughput and care about outcome statistics, not individual runs.


> *Likely follow-up:* A mean-and-podium gate would pass two distributions with the same mean but different tails -- and you sell CVaR tail-risk as a product feature. Doesn't that under-test the thing you optimise on? Steer: concede it squarely. The current test does not gate on CVaR or the full position distribution, so a tail divergence in Rust could slip through. The fix is a two-sample KS or a CVaR-delta assertion on the position array; until then I only trust Rust for mean/probability screening and re-run the shortlist's tail in Python.


**Q (KILLER). Resolve the conflict in your own claims: the engine makes Bahrain a 2-stop, but Norris ran a mechanical 6-stop. How much of the +7-position headline is real strategy versus a broken car, and does your ghost actually isolate that?**

Let me separate two different numbers, because the first draft blurred them. Crucially, BOTH runs pass the same focal_retire = his real last lap (lines 208-212). The headline +7, though, is a different and weaker quantity: it compares the AI's SIM finish against Norris's ACTUAL classified finish. > *Likely follow-up:* If he genuinely finished (not retired), then focal_retire is None and nothing cancels -- so the ghost delta is just AI-2-stop minus his-real-6-stop, which flatters the AI because a 6-stop is a pathological baseline no strategist would choose. ## ML, LIVE & LATENCY


**Q (warm-up). The Kalman filter (updater.py) uses R=0.09 and Q=diag(1e-3, 5e-5). Tell me what those mean operationally and why you fixed them by hand rather than fitting them.**

State is z=[a,b]: fresh-tyre fuel-corrected pace and degradation rate (s/lap), observation H=[1, tyre_age]. R=0.09 means sqrt(R)=0.3s of per-lap measurement noise — that's my prior for green-lap scatter (driver variation, micro track changes) after fuel-correction. Q is the random-walk process noise: drift_pace=1e-3 lets 'a' track the circuit rubbering in; drift_deg=5e-5 lets 'b' creep as tyre/track temps move. I fixed them rather than fitting for an honest reason: I only have ~10-20 usable laps per stint, so jointly fitting (R,Q) per stint would be badly identified — you can trade measurement noise against process noise and get the same likelihood. Hand-set, slightly conservative values give graceful behaviour (small Q means the filter won't chase a single noisy lap), and the synthetic-truth test (test_calibration_live.py::test_kalman_recovers_known_deg) confirms that with these values the filter recovers a planted b=0.08 to within 0.02 and the truth lands inside the 90% credible band. If I had a full season of stints I'd fit Q by maximum-likelihood / EM offline and freeze it; I haven't claimed to have done that.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'Show me you're not just getting lucky on the prior — what if prior_deg is wrong by 3x?' Steer: the test seeds prior_deg=0.0 (zero) and still recovers 0.08; the prior_var_deg=0.02 inflation means the prior is weak and washes out in ~6-8 laps. Concede early-stint estimates are prior-dominated.


**Q (warm-up). pit_advisor samples 400 draws from the posterior covariance to build the pit window instead of mean +- z*sigma on [a,b]. Justify the Monte-Carlo over the closed form.**

Because the map from (a,b) to the optimal pit lap is nonlinear and non-Gaussian, so a Gaussian credible interval on [a,b] does NOT give a correct interval on the pit lap. _remaining_time integrates the worn-tyre extrapolation, a pit loss, and a fresh-stint run, then argmins over candidate laps — that argmin is a step function of (a,b), and the posterior on [a,b] is a correlated bivariate Gaussian. Propagating mean+-sigma marginally would ignore the a-b correlation and would push through the nonlinearity wrong; you'd get a symmetric window when the real induced distribution on the pit lap is skewed and discrete. So I draw 400 (a,b) pairs from the full posterior covariance (pit_advisor.py line 102), re-solve the argmin for each, and take the 5th/95th percentiles of the resulting pit laps — that's the correct Bayesian push-forward through the optimiser. It's also robustly coded: if the covariance is non-PSD it falls back to point estimates (lines 103-104). 400 is enough for 5/95 percentiles to be stable to ~1 lap; I'd raise it if I wanted tighter tail quantiles, but the pit-lap distribution is effectively unimodal so 400 is fine.


> *Likely follow-up:* '400 draws for a 5th percentile means ~20 draws in the tail — is your window stable run to run?' Steer: seed is fixed (seed=0) so it's deterministic, but that hides MC noise rather than removing it; concede the 5/95 could wobble ~1 lap and I'd bump to 1-2k draws if the window drove an irreversible call.


**Q (warm-up). Feature normalizers are hardcoded — n_laps/70, pit_loss/30, focal_delta/3. New circuit with 70 laps and a 35s pit loss. What breaks, and why didn't you use a fitted scaler?**

Two normalisation layers, and only one is hardcoded — that distinction matters. The hardcoded divisors in features.py (70, 30, 3) are just to put raw features in roughly [-1,1] for the training circuits. On top of that, the network has a LEARNED standardiser: set_norm stores x_mean/x_std fit on the training split (model.py lines 33-38, applied at forward line 41), saved as buffers in the checkpoint. So the model IS partly data-driven-normalised. The failure mode for a 70-lap / 35s-pit-loss circuit: the hardcoded layer pushes n_laps/70=1.0 and pit_loss/30=1.17 — past the range the learned x_mean/x_std ever saw, so those features land in the tails of the standardiser and the MLP extrapolates into a regime its activations weren't trained on. It runs, but the prediction is untrustworthy. Why not a single fitted scaler end to end? Honestly, convenience — the hardcoded divisors made the feature vector human-readable in the dashboard, and every F1 circuit in the pool sits within ~50-60 laps and ~20-28s, so it never bound in practice. The clean fix is to drop the hardcoded divisors and let one StandardScaler (already effectively present via set_norm) own all normalisation, refit when the circuit pool changes, and add the OOD gate so a 70-lap query flags rather than silently extrapolates.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'If set_norm already standardises, the hardcoded divisors are redundant — are they actually doing anything, or is this dead code you're rationalising?' Steer: they're near-redundant; standardisation after a linear pre-divide is mathematically absorbed by x_std, so the divisors mostly aid readability. Concede they add no modelling value and could be removed — don't dress it up as a deliberate design.


**Q (core). You claim reoptimize() sweeps thousands of remaining plans in milliseconds, O(1) per plan. Derive the O(1) and give me the REAL evaluation count and wall time — not the headline.**

The O(1)-per-plan is real and comes from prefix sums: I precompute Fpre=cumsum(fuel) and CURpre=cumsum(current-tyre cost) once in O(n), so any contiguous-lap segment cost is a single index subtraction (Fpre[hi]-Fpre[lo-1]). A fresh stint cost is closed-form too — fresh_sum uses the triangular-number identity m(m+1)/2 for the sum of k1*age over a stint, so it's O(1), not a loop. But I won't oversell the count. I just measured it: at Bahrain (57 laps), lap 5 with two stops allowed is ~11,600 evaluations in ~14ms; lap 20 is ~5,800 in ~7ms; lap 40 is ~1,100 in ~1.2ms — pure Python. So the '~6k in ~7ms' headline is the MID-RACE case, and the worst case (early race) is closer to 14ms. The count shrinks as the race progresses because the 2-stop nest is O((n-lap)^2 x 9 compound-pairs). The legal()/alloc_ok() gates skip infeasible branches but I still increment n_eval before the gate, so n_eval is the gross enumeration, not the kept-plan count (kept plans are far fewer, then sorted O(M log M)). Bottom line: it's tens of milliseconds worst case, comfortably sub-frame for a once-per-lap live loop, and the math per plan genuinely is constant-time.


> *Likely follow-up:* '14ms in pure Python — what happens at Spa (44 laps but you said it scales with remaining laps) or if I allow 3 stops?' Steer: 3 stops would add an O(n^3) nest and blow up; I cap at max_more_stops=2 deliberately because 3-stop-from-here is rare and I'd switch to the DP optimiser. Concede the design choice, don't pretend it generalises free.


**Q (core). The surrogate has a heteroscedastic head — mean plus log-variance via Gaussian NLL. Why bother, and is the variance actually calibrated or just a number?**

Two heads, and I'll separate what's validated from what's aspirational. There's a pos_logvar head trained by Gaussian NLL (model.py line 44, clamped to [-6,6] for stability) and a separate head_std trained by MSE against the simulator's own outcome SPREAD (the std_position label). So pos_uncertainty = exp(0.5*logvar) is the surrogate's epistemic confidence in its mean prediction; std_pos is its estimate of the intrinsic race-outcome spread. Operationally the value is robustness ranking: if pit-lap-28 gives mean P8.1 with high predicted spread and pit-lap-30 gives P8.2 with low spread, the strategist should often take the more robust 8.2 — a point predictor hides that. On calibration: evaluate_surrogate computes uncertainty_coverage_1sigma (fraction of validation truths within +-1sigma of the mean head). I report it but I will NOT claim it's perfectly calibrated — a single coverage scalar near ~0.68 only tells you it's roughly right on average, not that it's sharp or correct in the tails. Honest position: the variance head is useful signal and trained properly, but I haven't done a full reliability-diagram calibration or tested coverage out-of-distribution, so I treat it as a relative robustness indicator, not a trustworthy absolute confidence interval.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'If you only checked +-1sigma coverage, your model could be over-confident in the tails and you'd never see it. Why does that not bite a live pit call?' Steer: concede it could; the mitigation is that live pit calls don't run off the surrogate alone — the surrogate sweeps the heatmap, but the final commit re-runs the Rust MC on the top candidates. Surrogate is triage, not the trigger.


**Q (core). Quantify the surrogate's real speedup honestly. Single inference, batch throughput, and the FULL live recommendation loop — on the hardware you actually have.**

Measured on this machine (CPU only — no CUDA; MPS is available but I run CPU for determinism): a single predict() is ~69us, and a batch of 2,400 strategies is ~0.72ms, i.e. ~3.3M strategies/s on CPU. The README's conservative '2,400 in ~2ms / ~1.1M/s' is if anything understated. > *Likely follow-up:* 'You said CPU for determinism — is the surrogate even the bottleneck, or is feature encoding?' Steer: encode() is pure-Python per-strategy and at 300-2400 strategies it's non-trivial; I haven't profiled encode vs forward separately. **Q (core). Robust gating (gate_z=4.0) versus just throwing out traffic laps. Walk me through the exact mechanism and why down-weighting beats deletion.**

Mechanism, precisely: each lap I compute the standardised innovation z = (y - Hz)/sqrt(S). If |z| > gate_z=4, I inflate the measurement noise to R_eff = R * (|z|/gate_z)^2 and recompute S with R_eff (updater.py lines 113-116). That makes the Kalman gain for that lap multiplicatively smaller — the lap still updates the state, but gently and in proportion to how far out it is; it's a soft Huber-style down-weight, not a hard reject. Note this is SEPARATE from the anomaly flag (anomaly_z=3.0), which only labels the lap for the dashboard and doesn't change the update. Why not delete traffic laps? Three reasons. First, deletion needs a classifier of what 'traffic' is, and a 3-4s loss can be perfectly legitimate degradation, a cold restart, or fuel — you'd be throwing away real signal on a guess. Second, deletion is brittle at the threshold; down-weighting degrades continuously, so a borderline 3.5-sigma lap isn't all-or-nothing. Third, keeping all laps with a soft weight means the filter self-heals: the synthetic test plants a +4s spike at age 12 and asserts the filter flags it AND fully recovers by age 15 with no contamination of later beliefs (test_calibration_live.py::test_kalman_flags_anomaly). On real data the gain over OLS is the modest-but-real ~0.84->0.78 RMSPE I quoted — robustness is why the online fit beats growing-window OLS, which has no such down-weighting and gets dragged by every outlier it ingests.


> *Likely follow-up:* '(|z|/4)^2 grows without bound — a 20-sigma garbage lap still moves the state a little. Why not cap it?' Steer: fair; at very large z the R_eff inflation makes the gain ~1/z^2 so influence vanishes asymptotically, but it never hits exactly zero. Concede a hard cap or a redescending (Tukey) weight would be cleaner for extreme outliers.

**Q (core). Walk me through your undercut/overcut model (_battle in field_model.py). A real pit wall watches gaps compress under SC and reads rivals' tyre temperatures. What does your model actually compute, and — be precise — has it ever been validated against real undercut outcomes?**

When I detect a rival is about to pit (their predicted pit lap from the live reoptimiser is within a 4-lap HORIZON), I compute: per_lap_gain × laps_out − current_gap, where per_lap_gain is my fresh pace (focal_a adjusted by the k0 compound offset, floored at 0.15 s/lap) minus the rival's worn pace (their Kalman fresh_pace + deg_rate × age). margin > 1.0s with ≥2 laps out flags a 'viable' jump. The fresh/worn paces come from the Kalman belief over fuel-corrected lap times — that's my proxy for the thermography a real wall has; I'm honest that I work from timing data, not tyre-surface temps. Now the validation question, because this is where I won't bluff: _battle itself has NO test asserting it called a real undercut correctly. The 0.13–0.29 r-values people sometimes attach to it are NOT undercut-prediction accuracy — they're the dirty-air proximity-fit r-values from a different module (calibrate_dirty_air), and I won't let them be read as validation of the battle logic. What IS validated is the chain underneath: the Kalman beats persistence/OLS on one-step lap-time RMSPE (0.37 vs 0.43/0.40s), and the full-field sim reconstructs Bahrain at Spearman 0.72. So _battle is a physically-sensible, deterministic margin off validated inputs — I'd present it to a strategist as a directional signal ('undercut on, ~2s in hand over 3 laps'), explicitly NOT as a calibrated probability. I also don't model gap compression INSIDE _battle; that lives in the simulator's SC bunching, and the live reoptimiser is what actually flips the pit call when a window opens.

**Q (core). Your ghost's allocation constraint (_infer_allocation) caps the AI at one set per compound above the field's max usage, capped at FIA totals. > *Likely follow-up:* 'OpenF1 laps arrive incomplete and out of order, with corrections — does ingest() tolerate a lap that arrives twice or a lap_time that gets revised?' Steer: concede ingest() assumes monotonic, one-shot laps (it mutates Kalman state in place), so a real poller needs idempotency/dedup and a re-process-on-correction layer before the seam; that layer doesn't exist yet.


**Q (KILLER). The server uses lru_cache on ~12 functions. What's the stale-cache failure mode trackside and how would you fix it for a genuinely live session?**

There are 12 lru_cache decorators in server.py. The subtle one is _model(circuit), maxsize=64: it caches the *built* RaceModel object. There's a partial decoupling that bites here — data/priors.py wraps load_priors() in its own lru_cache and clears it inside save_priors(), so a build_priors.py run does refresh the raw JSON cache. But _model() caches the assembled RaceModel downstream of that, and nothing invalidates it, so a mid-session recalibration is invisible until the process restarts. Same for _surrogate() (maxsize=1) after a retrain, and _scenarios()/_rivals() per circuit. There is no /api/reload endpoint anywhere in the codebase — I checked; the only cache_clear() call in the project is the one in save_priors() for the priors JSON. Worth noting these caches are keyed by circuit/grid/delta, so they're correct for a what-if tool; the staleness only matters once data changes under a running process, which is exactly the live case. The fix I'd ship: (1) a /api/reload endpoint, strategist-auth-gated, that calls cache_clear() on _model, _scenarios, _rivals, _surrogate; or better (2) fold a hash of priors.json plus model-version into the cache key so a recalibration auto-invalidates without an operator action. priors.json today carries a meta block (source, seasons) but no build timestamp or content hash, so step zero is stamping it. I'd pair the invalidation with a forced re-score, because otherwise the surrogate heatmap refreshes instantly while the Monte-Carlo recommendation lags it by its compute time.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'If you cache_clear() mid-race, the next request pays the full cold-start — rebuilding the model and a 400-scenario bank under a live deadline. How do you avoid a stall right when a strategist is reacting to an SC?' Steer: rebuild into a shadow cache off the hot path and atomically swap the reference, so no in-flight request eats the cold start; never clear-then-rebuild synchronously on the request thread.


**Q (KILLER). The README claims the surrogate hits R²=0.93 at ~1.1M strategies/s. Where does that number live, and what stops a fast wrong surrogate answer from driving a decision the Monte-Carlo would contradict?**

First, an honest correction to how that number is evidenced: R²=0.93 and 'mean error 1.0 finishing position' are metrics computed in surrogate/train.py (r2_pos, mae_pos on a held-out split) and quoted in the README — they are NOT asserted by the test suite. test_surrogate.py only checks feature-dim consistency, strategy legality, and output shapes/bounds; it never asserts R² or rank quality. So the accuracy claim rests on a training run, not a regression-guarded test, and I'd call that a real gap. On throughput: I measured ~0.95M strategies/s on a large batch here, so '~1.1M/s' is the right order, mildly optimistic. On the asymmetric-failure question: the design separates roles — the surrogate powers the instant heatmap (pit-lap x compound grid, sub-millisecond per cell; I measured a 47-cell heatmap at ~0.08ms total), while the robust recommendation goes through robust_optimize(), which scores the shortlist with the actual Monte-Carlo sim and returns the best plan plus a 'reason' (runner-up, gap, pit loss). The surrogate never gets the final word — it's a search prefilter; the MC is the arbiter. The genuine hole is the tail: train.py reports global R², but nothing checks that the surrogate's top-N *ranking* matches the MC's top-N, or that it's accurate for finishes 18-20. A surrogate that's biased in the tail would steer the heatmap a strategist eyeballs while the MC recommendation quietly disagrees — visible in the UI, but only if someone is looking. The fix is a rank-correlation test (Spearman of surrogate vs MC over ~1000 random strategies, assert >=0.95 on the top slice) wired into CI, plus flagging cells where surrogate and MC disagree by >0.5 positions.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'You say the MC is always the arbiter — but the heatmap is what the strategist stares at on the pit wall. If the surrogate is the one in front of a human, isn't IT the de-facto decision tool?' Steer: agree, and bite the bullet — for anything a human acts on directly, the surrogate needs the same rank-validation bar as the MC; either gate the heatmap behind an agreement check or annotate cells the MC hasn't confirmed.


**Q (KILLER). Trackside you get a lap roughly every 90 seconds and must re-optimise within a sub-second budget. Profile the live loop honestly — where does the time actually go, and how would you architect for a pit wall?**

I profiled the real components rather than guessing. Per lap, ingest() does: a Kalman update (O(1), ~1ms); pit_advisor.advise() with n_samples=400 — this is the real cost at ~37ms median, 40ms p95, because it re-solves the full remaining-time curve for every one of 400 posterior draws in pure Python; and live_optimizer.reoptimize(), which is NOT O(1) overall despite the docstring. The prefix sums make each *stint's* cost O(1), but the 2-stop sweep is O(n^2 * |compounds|^2): I measured 7,504 plans evaluated in ~8ms mid-race (lap 15) and 13,531 plans in ~16ms early (lap 1, the worst case). So the docstring's 'thousands of strategies in a millisecond' overstates by ~10x — the honest figure is single-digit-to-low-teens milliseconds, and the dominant cost in the loop is actually the Bayesian pit window, not the sweep. Even summed (~1ms + ~37ms + ~16ms ≈ 55ms worst case for one car) we're comfortably inside a per-lap budget for one driver — the surrogate forward pass, which an earlier design note feared at '50ms', is actually ~0.07ms, so it's a non-issue. The scaling risk is the field: field_model.py runs this stack for all 20 drivers *sequentially* (no threading/async/pool anywhere — I checked), so a full-field pass is ~20x the single-car cost, ~1s, which blows the budget if you do it synchronously every lap. Architecture: (a) pre-warm at session start — load 20 car models, a fixed seeded 400-scenario bank, the surrogate, at pit-lane open; (b) the cheap win is to cut advise()'s 400-sample window — vectorise the remaining-time curve over draws (it's currently a Python loop) or drop to ~100 samples, which alone reclaims ~30ms; (c) shard by driver across workers since the per-car stacks are independent, with results streamed to the wall; (d) under an SC, react with reoptimize alone (cheap-window pit logic, ~8ms) and defer the expensive Bayesian window to the next green lap.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'You quote median and p95 — but a pit-wall budget is about the worst case under load, and Python has the GIL. If you shard 20 drivers across threads, do you actually get parallelism?' Steer: concede threads won't help CPU-bound pure-Python work under the GIL — it has to be processes (or the Rust path) for true parallelism; the per-driver independence makes process-sharding clean, but it's not built and the GIL is the reason naive threading would disappoint.


**Q (KILLER). The ghost counterfactual runs the focal driver's real strategy vs the AI's under the real SC timeline with CRN, against rivals on their actual strategies. How do you know the AI strategy isn't just getting lucky on safety-car timing?**

You've found the real limitation, and the headline number reflects it. ghost.py runs the focal car twice against a *fixed* field — every rival on their real committed strategy and calibrated pace, real retirements forced via retire_lap, the actual SC/VSC/RED timeline rebuilt lap-by-lap from TrackStatus — once on the driver's actual strategy, once with the AI making live pit calls via _ai_policy (which boxes when reoptimize says BOX NOW, and grabs the cheap stop inside an SC window). Because it's CRN — same scenario seeds, only lap noise varies — the real-vs-AI delta is clean apples-to-apples. But I cannot, from this single counterfactual, decompose the AI's gain into 'out-strategised the field' versus 'caught the SC at the right moment', and I shouldn't pretend otherwise. The code today builds exactly one ghost per (year, gp, driver); none of the ablations that would separate luck from skill are exposed. To actually answer the question I'd add: (a) an SC-ablation run with the real timeline forced green (regime all 'green') — if the AI's gap shrinks, it was SC-timing luck; if it holds, the edge is base degradation/compound choice; (b) a rivals-also-AI variant (field_model already runs the live engine for all 20 cars, so the machinery exists) to test whether the edge survives when everyone optimises; (c) a nominal-field baseline (all rivals on a fixed 1-stop) to isolate strategy from raw car pace. I'd return the decomposition explicitly — finish_ai, finish_ai_no_sc, sc_value_s — so the verdict reads 'SC timing was worth N positions, strategy worth M' instead of one blended number. And the honest caveat that's already in the project: the sim carries a few-positions absolute error, so the real-vs-AI *delta* is the trustworthy quantity, not either absolute finish — and the Bahrain +7 headline is inflated by a broken real car (a mechanical 6-stop), so the pure-strategy gain is smaller than the number suggests.


> *Likely follow-up:* 'Your CRN compares two focal strategies on a FROZEN rival field — but if the AI undercuts a rival, that rival would have reacted and re-pitted. Doesn't a static field overstate the AI's gain?' Steer: concede frozen rivals are the core methodological weakness — it grants the AI an undercut the real pit wall would have covered; the rivals-also-AI / best-response variant is the fix, and until it's built the ghost is an optimistic upper bound on strategic gain, which I'd label as such.
